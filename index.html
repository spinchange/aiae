What is the AIAE?

The idea. Every share of stock, every bond and every dollar of cash in the economy has to be held by someone. Add up the market value of all stocks, divide by the value of all stocks plus all bonds and cash, and you get the share of the average investor's portfolio that sits in stocks. That ratio is the Aggregate (or Average) Investor Allocation to Equities. Bonds and cash are measured from the other side of the ledger, as the debts of the economy's real borrowers: households, nonfinancial companies, the federal government, state and local governments, and the rest of the world.

Where it comes from. The pseudonymous blogger "Jesse Livermore" introduced it on the Philosophical Economics blog on December 20, 2013, in a post titled "The Single Greatest Predictor of Future Stock Market Returns." Back to 1952, it lined up with subsequent 10-year stock returns more closely than the usual valuation measures, including Shiller's CAPE. In a follow-up post, Livermore said the title was a deliberate exaggeration and cautioned that historical fits like this owe a lot to curve fitting.

Who popularized it. Mark Hulbert, a longtime MarketWatch columnist and the founder of Hulbert Ratings, has written about it many times since 2018. He has reported that it had the best track record of the long-term valuation indicators he tested (a recent column). One difference matters here: Hulbert's columns track a household-only version, U.S. households' equity share of their financial assets. This page tracks Livermore's original economy-wide formula, which covers all investors, including foreign holders of U.S. assets.

Why it's interesting. It looks at stocks from the side of supply and demand, not earnings. Companies issue relatively little new stock, while ongoing borrowing keeps adding bonds and cash to investors' portfolios. If investors want to hold a steady share in stocks, stock prices have to rise over time to absorb the growing pile of everything else. When the allocation is already high, investors have less room to add stocks and less cash and bonds to buy them with. Historically, that has meant lower returns over the following decade. When it is low, as in mid-1982 at its all-time low of about 22%, there is room for prices to rise. Because it measures how fully invested people already are, it acts as a contrarian gauge of investor positioning.

Why the detrended view. The AIAE's "normal" level may have shifted upward over the decades. Retirement accounts, cheap index funds and an older, wealthier population all plausibly raise how much stock investors are comfortable holding. Measured against its full history, a reading can look extreme. Measured against the last 20 years, it may look only elevated. In walk-forward tests, the AIAE has forecast 10-year nominal returns well since 1980. For returns above cash, though, it has run too pessimistic since the late 1990s, which is consistent with that kind of drift. Read it as a measure of how stretched investor positioning is, not as a precise forecast.

AIAE = (NCBEILQ027S + FBCELLQ027S) ÷ (those + BCNSDODNS + CMDEBT + FGSDODNS + SLGSDODNS + DODFFSWCMI). Detrended = AIAE minus its trailing 80-quarter average (pre-1952 data is annual and is included in early averages). Source: Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial Accounts via FRED.
